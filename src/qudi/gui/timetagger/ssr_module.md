Software Architecture and Implementation for Single-Shot Readout of Tin-Vacancy Centers in Diamond Utilizing the Qudi FrameworkIntroduction to Solid-State Spin-Photon Interfaces and Group-IV DefectsThe development of scalable quantum information processing architectures and long-distance quantum networks relies fundamentally on the availability of robust, uniform, and optically accessible solid-state qubits. Over the past decade, atomic-scale defects in wide-bandgap semiconductors, particularly color centers in diamond, have emerged as preeminent candidates for these applications. While the nitrogen-vacancy ($NV^-$) center in diamond has historically dominated the field due to its room-temperature spin coherence and initialization capabilities, its utility in scalable quantum networks is fundamentally limited by its low emission into the zero-phonon line (ZPL) and its high susceptibility to electrical noise, which degrades optical stability.To overcome these limitations, the quantum optics community has increasingly turned to group-IV color centers in diamond, encompassing the silicon ($SiV^-$), germanium ($GeV^-$), tin ($SnV^-$), and lead ($PbV^-$) vacancy centers. These defects share a $D_{3d}$ point group symmetry, characterized by an interstitial impurity atom positioned halfway between two adjacent missing carbon atoms. This specific crystallographic configuration preserves an inversion center, rendering the defect's electronic states insensitive to first-order local electric field fluctuations—a property crucial for integrating these emitters into nanophotonic structures without suffering from severe spectral diffusion.Among the group-IV defects, the negatively charged tin-vacancy center ($SnV^-$) presents a highly favorable, orthogonal combination of optical and spin properties. The electronic structure of the $SnV^-$ center features orbital doublets for both the ground ($E_g$) and excited ($E_u$) states. Strong spin-orbit coupling splits each of these doublets into two Kramers pairs, resulting in four distinct optical transitions (labeled A, B, C, and D) at cryogenic temperatures. The ground-state splitting of the $SnV^-$ center is exceptionally large, measuring approximately $\Delta_g \approx 850$ GHz, which can be further modified up to $1300$ GHz via engineered strain. This massive splitting is a pivotal characteristic, as it severely suppresses phonon-mediated thermal dephasing between the orbital branches. Consequently, the $SnV^-$ center can maintain millisecond-scale spin coherence times at temperatures between 1 Kelvin and 4 Kelvin. This temperature regime is highly advantageous because it falls within the operational capacity of standard helium-4 cryostats, which possess ample cooling power to mitigate the substantial heat load introduced by the continuous microwave driving required for rapid quantum logic gates.Furthermore, the $SnV^-$ center exhibits brilliant, spectrally stable emission. Its zero-phonon line (ZPL) is located at approximately 619 to 620 nm, with a high Debye-Waller factor of roughly 57%, indicating that a majority of the emitted photons are channeled into the coherent ZPL rather than the broad phonon sideband. The quantum efficiency of the defect is estimated to be near 80%, and it demonstrates lifetime-limited optical transitions with linewidths as narrow as 18 MHz, corresponding to the transform limit for a radiative lifetime of approximately 7.6 nanoseconds.Despite these exceptional characteristics, the utilization of group-IV centers has historically been hindered by a critical trade-off between the optical stability required for readout and the capacity for high-fidelity microwave spin control. The very spin-orbit coupling that provides thermal protection also suppresses the magnetic dipole transitions necessary for conventional quantum control. However, recent experimental breakthroughs have demonstrated that this limitation can be entirely circumvented for the $SnV^-$ center. By carefully applying a static magnetic field aligned at a specific angle relative to the defect's symmetry axis, in conjunction with moderate lattice strain, researchers have unlocked the ability to perform both rapid microwave spin control and high-fidelity single-shot readout (SSR) simultaneously. The software implementation required to execute these complex, tightly synchronized experimental protocols utilizing high-resolution timing hardware and the open-source Qudi framework forms the core subject of this comprehensive report.The Physical Framework of Single-Shot ReadoutSingle-shot readout is a foundational capability for advanced quantum information protocols, including error correction, deterministic quantum teleportation, and measurement-based quantum computing. Mathematically, SSR is defined as a projective measurement process capable of distinguishing between two orthogonal quantum states—typically denoted as the bright ground state $|0\rangle$ and the dark excited state $|1\rangle$—within a single execution of the experimental sequence.Spin-Selective Optical Transitions and CyclicityThe physical mechanism underlying SSR in the $SnV^-$ center relies on spin-selective resonant optical excitation. When an external magnetic field is applied, the spin degeneracy of the Kramers pairs is lifted via the Zeeman effect. If the magnetic field is oriented perfectly along the defect's symmetry axis, optical transitions strictly preserve the spin state. However, to enable microwave control, the magnetic field is intentionally misaligned. This deliberate misalignment introduces a mismatch between the quantization axes of the ground and excited spin states, thereby allowing for both spin-preserving (spin-cycling) transitions (labeled A1 and B2) and spin-flipping transitions (labeled A2 and B1).During an SSR protocol, a resonant laser is tuned specifically to one of the spin-preserving transitions, typically the A1 transition. If the electron spin is in the resonant state (e.g., $|\downarrow\rangle$), it will continuously cycle between the ground and excited states, scattering a stream of fluorescence photons. Conversely, if the electron spin is in the off-resonant state (e.g., $|\uparrow\rangle$), the laser cannot excite the transition, and the defect remains entirely dark.The fundamental efficiency of this readout mechanism is governed by the optical cyclicity, denoted as $\Lambda$. Cyclicity is defined as the ratio of the probability of a spin-preserving radiative decay to the probability of a spin-flipping radiative decay. A higher cyclicity means that the defect can scatter more photons before an unwanted spin-flip occurs, which would otherwise optically pump the electron into the dark state and prematurely terminate the readout signal. Through precise tuning of the magnetic field angle and amplitude, the cyclicity of the $SnV^-$ center can be engineered to exceed $\Lambda \approx 2.5 \times 10^3$, changing by three orders of magnitude depending on the field orientation. This massive cyclicity is the physical prerequisite that makes SSR possible in the $SnV^-$ platform, allowing repeated readout without significant spin-flip probability.Spectral Diffusion and Charge Resonance ChecksA persistent challenge with solid-state emitters is their susceptibility to charge state instability and spectral diffusion. The $SnV^-$ center can undergo photo-induced ionization, transitioning from its negatively charged state to a dark state, such as $SnV^{2-}$ or $SnV^0$. This phenomenon, colloquially known as "blinking," is catastrophic for SSR fidelity because a charge-state transition mimics the absence of fluorescence characteristic of the off-resonant spin state. Furthermore, fluctuations in the local electrostatic environment can induce Stark shifts that detune the defect's ZPL away from the resonant readout laser, leading to the same erroneous dark signal.To mitigate these issues, state-of-the-art SSR protocols incorporate a Charge Resonance Check (CRC) immediately prior to every sequence execution. The CRC is a pre-selection mechanism involving a brief, weak optical probe designed to verify that the defect is both in the correct charge state and strictly on resonance with the excitation laser. The scattered photons during this probe window are counted; if the collected photon number $N_{crc}$ meets or exceeds a predefined threshold, the defect is deemed ready, and the subsequent quantum logic and SSR pulses proceed. If the CRC fails, the experimental logic discards the iteration and typically triggers a charge-reset protocol utilizing high-power, off-resonant green (e.g., 515 nm or 517 nm) or blue lasers to re-initialize the defect before attempting the sequence again.The implementation of CRC pre-selection has a profound impact on measurement statistics. Unconditional single-shot readout of the $SnV^-$ center typically yields a fidelity of approximately 87.4%. However, by post-selecting only those cycles that successfully pass the CRC, the conditional fidelity of the readout can be elevated to an exceptional 98.5%.Quantifying Single-Shot Readout FidelityThe fidelity of the readout process is quantified by analyzing the statistical distribution of the collected photons. Over hundreds of thousands of measurement cycles, the integrated photon counts form a bimodal distribution. The peak centered near zero photons corresponds to the dark state ($|1\rangle$), while the peak at a higher photon count corresponds to the bright state ($|0\rangle$).A discrimination threshold, $n_{th}$, is established to classify the measurement outcomes. The readout fidelity, $F_r$, is mathematically expressed as:$$F_r = 1 - \frac{e_0 + e_1}{2}$$Here, $e_0$ represents the probability of erroneously classifying a true bright state as a dark state. This error is primarily driven by poor photon collection efficiency and premature spin-flips due to finite cyclicity. The Qudi framework, an open-source, modular, multi-operating system suite written in Python 3, was specifically engineered to address this challenge. Qudi provides a highly structured environment that enforces a strict separation of concerns, ensuring that the software remains maintainable, extensible, and adaptable to rapid changes in laboratory instrumentation.The Decoupled Three-Layer HierarchyThe foundational philosophy of Qudi is its rigid abstraction of functionality into three distinct layers: Hardware, Logic, and Graphical User Interface (GUI).The Hardware Layer: Modules in this layer serve as the fundamental drivers. They encapsulate the proprietary, vendor-specific APIs of the physical devices, such as the Swabian Instruments libraries or National Instruments DAQmx calls. To guarantee flexibility, Qudi enforces the use of interface classes. Hardware modules inherit from abstract parent classes (e.g., qudi.interface.pulser.PulserInterface) that define a standardized set of methods. Because of this abstraction, the upper logic layers never "know" what specific brand of instrument is connected; they simply call generic methods like start_sequence() or get_counts(). This design renders the interchanging of hardware modules completely transparent to the experimental logic layer.The Logic Layer: Logic modules function as the core intellectual engines of the application. They perform data processing, orchestrate complex state machines, and coordinate the activities of multiple hardware modules to execute a coherent experiment. Logic modules are strictly permitted to communicate only with modules of the same category or with the abstracted hardware interfaces. They receive high-level parameters, compute necessary algorithms, push commands to the hardware layer, and retrieve raw data for statistical analysis.The GUI Layer: The GUI modules provide user-friendly, interactive control panels for the experiments. Constructed using PyQt5 and designed via the Qt Designer software (which exports .ui files imported at runtime), these modules facilitate data visualization and parameter adjustment. Crucially, GUI modules are entirely prohibited from communicating directly with hardware modules. They only interact with Logic modules. This decoupling ensures that Qudi can operate in a "headless" state, allowing experiments to be entirely scripted and automated via Jupyter Notebooks or background task runners without any graphical overhead.Configuration and Module InstantiationThe dynamic instantiation and linking of these layers are governed by YAML-structured configuration files (.cfg). A configuration file declares which specific modules are to be loaded and explicitly defines their interconnections via a connect directive. For example, the configuration file maps the SequenceGeneratorLogic to the specific Swabian Pulse Streamer hardware module, and the PulsedMeasurementLogic to the Swabian Time Tagger module. During startup, Qudi strictly adheres to a dependency resolution algorithm, ensuring that hardware modules are fully initialized and stabilized before the logic modules attempt to bind to them, thereby preventing runtime race conditions.Logic Modules for Pulsed ExperimentsThe implementation of SSR inherently involves pulsed measurements. Qudi abstracts the complexity of pulsed experiments into a highly specialized ecosystem comprising several interrelated logic modules:SequenceGeneratorLogic: This module is responsible for the mathematical construction of the pulse sequences. It translates abstract representations of pulses (e.g., durations, amplitudes, analog waveforms) into the massive, high-speed digital and analog sample arrays required by the pulse generator hardware.PulsedMeasurementLogic: This module acts as the synchronization hub. It manages the counting hardware (e.g., the Time Tagger), triggers the sequence execution, retrieves the raw photon count data, and delegates the analysis of that data to dynamically loaded analysis routines.PulsedMasterLogic: Introduced in newer versions of Qudi, this module serves as a unified interface to decouple function calls between the SequenceGeneratorLogic and PulsedMeasurementLogic via Qt Signals. Because pulsed measurements are often invoked by custom scripts concurrently with GUI interactions, this signal-based decoupling is a crucial feature to ensure thread safety and prevent GUI freezing during long integrations.Hardware Orchestration: Swabian Instruments EcosystemHigh-fidelity single-shot readout of the $SnV^-$ center requires extraordinary temporal precision. The optical and microwave excitation pulses must be defined with nanosecond accuracy to prevent unwanted spin evolution, while the arrival times of individual fluorescence photons must be recorded with picosecond resolution to extract precise statistics and filter out detector dark counts. In the context of the $SnV^-$ SSR implementation, the Qudi hardware module initializes an instance of CountBetweenMarkers configured to listen to the marker channels driven by the Pulse Streamer. This architectural choice is critical: it guarantees that any detector dark counts, microwave-induced fluorescence artifacts, or scattering generated during the off-resonant charge-reset pulses are hardware-filtered and never contaminate the pristine SSR integration array.For advanced applications, such as synchronizing multiple $SnV^-$ nodes separated by large distances in a quantum network, the software must leverage the TimeTaggerNetwork functionality. By utilizing a 10 MHz reference clock and a 1PPS (Pulse Per Second) timing signal—often distributed via White Rabbit precision timing protocols—the Qudi software can seamlessly merge time-tag streams from remote devices across a network, correcting for the inherent frequency drift of independent local oscillators to maintain sub-nanosecond synchronization across the entire experimental apparatus.Designing the Pulse Sequence in SoftwareThe logical execution of the single-shot readout protocol begins in the SequenceGeneratorLogic module. The user must program this module to construct a specific repeating pulse block ensemble. A single cycle of the $SnV^-$ measurement sequence comprises several distinct, sequential phases that must be precisely defined in the software dictionaries.Phase 1: The Charge Resonance Check (CRC)The cycle initiates with the CRC window to combat spectral diffusion and blinking. The SequenceGeneratorLogic commands the Pulse Streamer to fire a start marker to the Time Tagger, immediately followed by activating the digital channel corresponding to the 619 nm resonant laser AOM for a duration typically spanning 5 to 10 microseconds. The laser is then deactivated, and a stop marker is sent. If the Time Tagger registers a photon count during this window that exceeds the software-defined threshold $N_{threshold}$, the logic permits the cycle's subsequent data to be validated.Phase 2: Spin InitializationFollowing the CRC, the electron spin must be deterministically initialized. This is achieved via a high-power optical pumping pulse on the resonant transition, driving the defect into the desired ground state (e.g., $|\uparrow\rangle$). If specific superposition states are required for calibration or quantum sensing applications, this optical pumping is immediately followed by specific microwave pulses.Phase 3: Microwave ManipulationThe software then defines the quantum logic operations. Depending on the specific experiment, this could involve a simple $\pi$-pulse to invert the spin state, a $\pi/2$-pulse to create a superposition for a Ramsey interferometry measurement, or a complex sequence of multiple $\pi$-pulses designed for dynamical decoupling (such as a CPMG sequence) to protect the spin state from the local nuclear spin bath. These operations are executed by pulsing the analog channels of the Pulse Streamer (if direct synthesis is used) or by digitally gating an external microwave source.Phase 4: Single-Shot ReadoutThe sequence concludes with the SSR window. The Pulse Streamer generates a second pair of start and stop markers to define a new integration window for the CountBetweenMarkers instance. Simultaneously, a moderate-power pulse from the 619 nm resonant laser is applied for a precisely optimized duration, $\tau$.The optimization of the readout duration $\tau$ and the optical power $p$ is a critical software calibration routine. The $SnV^-$ center is subject to measurement-induced dephasing. The dephasing rate is highly sensitive to the normalized optical power, $p / p_{sat}$. Below saturation, $\Gamma_\phi$ rises linearly with applied power. from TimeTagger import CountBetweenMarkers
import time

def extract_ssr_counts(self, tagger_device, detector_ch, start_ch, end_ch, num_cycles):
    """
    Custom extraction logic using the Time Tagger API for SSR.
    """
    # Set up the CountBetweenMarkers measurement
    cbm = CountBetweenMarkers(tagger_device, detector_ch, start_ch, end_ch, num_cycles)
    cbm.start()
    
    # PulsedMeasurementLogic commands the Pulse Streamer 
    # to execute the pulse sequence, driving optical/MW channels
    self.hardware.pulser.start_sequence()
    
    # Asynchronously poll the Time Tagger until all readouts are captured
    while not cbm.ready():
        time.sleep(0.01) 
        
    # Retrieve the integrated photon counts per readout window
    raw_ssr_counts = cbm.getData()
    
    return raw_ssr_counts
